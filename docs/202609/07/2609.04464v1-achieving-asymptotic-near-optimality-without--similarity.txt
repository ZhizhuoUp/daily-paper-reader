# Achieving Asymptotic Near-Optimality Without Delta-Similarity 

Michael Moncton and Eric Frew 

Abstract —Sampling-based motion planning algorithms are a popular class of trajectory planning algorithm due to their speed in complex, high-dimensional environments and ability to handle kinodynamic constraints, specifically through the use of forward dynamics propagation. Many such planners claim to achieve asymptotic near-optimality by proving the almost sure sampling of trajectories that are close to an optimal trajectory in the state space, known as δ-similar trajectories. This paper shows that the proof behind asymptotic δ-similarity relies on an unstated assumption that δ-similar trajectory segments will always be kept once sampled. This assumption does not hold in general. A problematic case, referred to as “crowding out,” is described, where locally low-cost paths prevent trajectories that are δ-similar to the optimal trajectory from being added to the tree. It is shown, however, that asymptotic near-optimality guarantees can still be achieved without guarantees of δ-similar solution trajectories when crowding out is properly accounted for. An example environment and system are provided where crowding out is shown to occur, demonstrating a scenario where inductively sampling a δ-similar solution trajectory is impossible. 

Index Terms —Motion planning, kinodynamic planning, sampling-based algorithms, asymptotic near-optimality, asymp-totic optimality 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Kinodynamic sampling-based motion planning (SBMP) al-gorithms have been a mainstay in robotics because of their ability to find feasible paths quickly in high-dimensional envi-ronments with complex dynamic constraints [1], [2], [3]. Some kinodynamic SBMP algorithms are able to converge towards optimal solution paths over time, a property called asymptotic optimality [4], [5], [6]. Asymptotically near-optimal (ANO) SBMP algorithms converge similarly, but are only guaranteed to reach costs within a bounded range of an optimal solution [7], [8]. To generate such near-optimal trajectories quickly, dynamics can be forward-propagated to generate a trajectory tree, which is then pruned to maintain a tree that increases in quality over time, such as in the Stable Sparse RRT (SST) algorithm [9]. In addition to improving the quality of the trajectory tree, the pruning process also sparsifies the trajectory tree, reducing the computation time of many planner subroutines. Many such sparse algorithms base proofs of near-optimality on the seminal work of the SST algorithm [10], [11]. In [9], solution paths that are exist within a distance of at most δ

from an optimal solution trajectory π∗ are shown to be near-optimal solution paths. An example of such a trajectory is the blue trajectory in Figure 1, πδs . The proof of the ANO property of SST implicitly assumes that δ-similar trajectory segments will always be added to the trajectory tree once sampled. This paper shows that this assumption is not true in general and that low-cost non-δ-similar nodes can permanently dominate regions near the optimal solution path, such as the segment

The proof as originally structured therefore cannot conclude that δ-similar nodes will be inductively added to the tree and the ANO property cannot be established without additional logic. This work provides a new proof that shows that δ-similar trajectories are not always guaranteed to be sampled, but that algorithmic near-optimality can still be achieved. By tracking the existence of near-optimal nodes directly rather than relying on the existence of δ-similar nodes, the induction argument is repaired without requiring the stronger and generally unattain-able claim of sampling δ-similar solution trajectories. As a result, it is shown that non-δ-similar trajectories can be near-optimal. A representative example is provided that illustrates the crowding-out effect, the guaranteed rejection of δ-similar trajectory segments, and non-δ-similar near-optimal solution trajectories. II. B. Definitions 

Definition 1 (Trajectory) . A trajectory π is a mapping π(t) : [0 , t π ] → Xa generated by integrating Equation 1 with a control function u(t).

Trajectory segments that are sampled and not pruned are added to a trajectory tree G. The endpoints of such trajectories are vertices on the tree where vertex i is represented as vi. The location of vi in the state space is represented as xi.One common method of generating large, complex solution trajectories is to sample smaller, feasible trajectory segments using randomized control functions and concatenating them to form larger, piecewise trajectories. Trajectory segments are sampled with a propagation time tprop ∈ (0 , T prop ). Selecting an appropriate value for Tprop requires some domain specific knowledge. Excessively small values will generate trajectory segments that do not exit localized sparsity neighborhoods, while excessively large values produce highly collision-prone trajectory segments.

Around any path with positive dynamic clearance, there exists a set of feasible paths which have a non-zero probability of being sampled. As in [9], it is assumed that in all problems addressed in this work there exists an optimal solution trajectory with non-zero dynamic clearance. Notably, it is not possible to create feasible paths to or from every point in a covering ball that includes an obstacle. Therefore, dynamic clearance will always be less than or equal to obstacle clearance, which is defined as the minimum distance from any point on a trajectory to any point in collision with an obstacle. The analysis in [9] uses the concept of δ-robustness, which is the minimum of dynamic clearance and obstacle clearance for a path. As obstacle clearance cannot be less than dynamic clearance, dynamic clearance and δ-robustness can be conceptually used interchangeably. 

Definition 3 (Near-Optimal Kinodynamic δ-Robust Motion Planning) . Given an initial state x0, a goal region Xgoal ,system constraints including dynamic constraints in Equation 1 and the admissible space Xa, and that an optimal solution path with dynamic clearance δ ∈ R>0 and cost c∗ exists, find a solution trajectory π such that π(0) = x0 and π(tπ ) ∈ Xgoal ,and cost (π) ≤ h(c∗, δ ).

The function h(c∗, δ ) bounds the difference in cost between a trajectory found by a planning algorithm and the cost of the optimal solution trajectory. In contrast, many feasible planning algorithms have an unbounded cost difference [1], [13]. The near-optimal δ-robust motion planning problem assumes that there exists an optimal trajectory with positive dynamic clearance. 

Definition 4 (Asymptotic Near-Optimality) . An algorithm is asymptotically near-optimal if the cost of the best path found by the algorithm approaches a value bounded by a function 

h(c∗, δ ) as the number of algorithm iterations approaches infinity. 

Near optimality for a motion planning algorithm is a guar-antee of probabilistic performance as the algorithm is allowed to run. For algorithms solving the problem in Definition 3, the probability that an algorithm will sample a solution trajectory with a sufficiently low cost is one as the number of iterations goes to infinity. 

Definition 5 (Near-Optimal Node) . A node x is near-optimal if there exists a near-optimal solution trajectory πN O such that x ∈ πN O .

Near-optimal nodes have the potential to be propagated into near-optimal solution trajectories. The creation of such nodes is essential to the generation of near-optimal solution trajectories. 

Definition 6 (δ-Similar Trajectories, Definition 3 in [9]) .

Trajectories π and π′ are δ-similar if, for a continuous non-decreasing scaling function σ : [0 , t π ] → [0 , t π′ ], π′(σ(t)) ∈Bδ (π(t)) .

For trajectories to be δ-similar they must exist within a scaled time-parameterized state-space ball of each other for the entire duration of the trajectory. In this work, trajectories will be referred to as δ-similar if they are δ-similar to π∗.In [9], paths that are δ-similar to an optimal solution trajectory π∗ are shown to be δ-robustly near-optimal. A δ-similar trajectory therefore is a solution to the near-optimal δ-robust motion planning problem. Finding δ-similar trajectories, however, is sufficient but not necessary for finding a near-optimal trajectory. 

Definition 7 (δ-Similar Node) . A node v is a δ-similar node if its source trajectory is δ-similar to a subset of π∗.

The source trajectory for v is the trajectory originating at x0

and terminating in v. If this trajectory is δ-similar to a section of π∗, also from x0 to some point near v, then v can be considered a δ-similar node. Defining partial δ-similarity for nodes and trajectories will be useful in examining individual algorithm iterations in the proof section later in this work.

Proving asymptotic near-optimality is accomplished via an induction proof. Let Bδ (π∗) be the covering ball sequence con-structed around the optimal trajectory π∗ which has dynamic clearance δ. The induction proof will show that as the number of algorithm iterations approaches infinity, trajectory segments will almost surely be propagated from one covering ball into the next for each ball in the sequence. While individual segments may not connect to each other or may be rejected entirely by the algorithm, the act of their sampling proves the existence of near-optimal nodes in the next ball in the sequence. By comparing such trajectory segments to possible 

δ-similar trajectories, it can be proven that even non-δ-similar trajectories can lead to near-optimal solutions.

C. Propagating Over Covering Balls 

It is important to acknowledge that increasing the maximum propagation time allows for propagation through the crowded-out region, ignoring the crowding out effect. However, for a given Tprop , it is always possible to create an adversarial en-vironment where propagation through the crowded-out region is impossible. The potential solution of increasing Tprop is also highly impractical, as Tprop would have to scale with the size of crowded-out regions, which are not initially known or even bounded in size. Increasing Tprop also reduces the probability that any given trajectory segment is collision-free, reducing performance. Carried far enough, as Tprop 

approaches levels greater than or equal to tπ∗ , the algorithm reduces to attempting to solve the entire planning problem with a single propagation. Such behavior is exactly what sampling-based iterative motion planning is meant to avoid. V. C ONCLUSIONS 

Many forward-propagating sparse SBMP algorithms prove asymptotic near-optimality through the claim that a solution that is δ-similar to the optimal solution path will almost surely be found given sufficient iterations. However, in some situations, δ-similar trajectories are guaranteed to be pruned from the tree. This work provides a refined proof of asymptotic near-optimality without claiming guarantees of sampling δ-similar paths. The new proof applies generally to similar sparse algorithms that utilize the claim of almost-surely sampling δ-similar trajectories. An example planning problem is provided where δ-similar trajectories are guaranteed to be rejected from certain regions near the optimal path. In such an environment, it is possible to sample near-optimal paths, despite the guar-anteed pruning of δ-similar paths. REFERENCES [1] S. M. LaValle and J. J.
