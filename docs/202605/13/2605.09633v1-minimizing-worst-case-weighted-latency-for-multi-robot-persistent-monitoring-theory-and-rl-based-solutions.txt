Abstract 

We study multi-robot persistent monitoring on weighted graphs, where node weights encode monitoring priorities and edge weights encode travel distances. The goal is to design joint robot trajectories that minimize the worst-case weighted latency across all nodes over an infinite time horizon. The widely adopted worst-case latency objective evaluates team performance over the entire time horizon and therefore may fail to distinguish strategies with poor transient behavior but strong asymptotic performance. To address this limitation, we propose a family of tail-performance objectives that generalize the standard objective and study the resulting functional optimization problems. We establish several key theoretical properties, including the existence of optimal strategies, relationships among the proposed objectives and their corresponding optimization problems, approximation by periodic solutions to arbitrary accuracy, and reductions to event-driven decision models with discretized waiting times. Building on these results, we construct an equivalent event-driven Markov decision process (MDP), called the Tail Worst-case Latency-Optimizing Markov Decision Process (TWLO-MDP), which reformulates the tail-performance objective as a standard average-reward criterion. We then develop reinforcement-learning-based solution methods for the TWLO-MDP and introduce the multi-robot monitoring benchmark (M2Bench), a unified platform that supports the evaluation and comparison of heuristic and learning-based monitoring algorithms. Experiments on synthetic and realistic monitoring scenarios show that our methods effectively reduce the worst-case weighted latency and outperform representative baselines. 

Keywords 

Multi-robot monitoring, Markov decision process, reinforcement learning, benchmarking platform 

## 1 Introduction 

Persistent monitoring is a key application area for many real-world robotic systems. Typical examples include urban crime surveillance (Palma-Borda et al. 2026), post-disaster area monitoring (Kumar et al. 2022), anomaly detection (Witwicki et al. 2017), ocean monitoring (Smith et al. 2011) and forest fire detection (Momeni et al. 2022). In these scenarios, a team of robots repeatedly visits a set of important locations so that abnormal events can be detected in a timely manner. A widely used performance measure is the latency, which measures the amount of time a location remains unvisited. At its core, this task concerns the design of long-term motion strategies for multiple robots moving on a graph. Robots traverse edges representing physical paths, subject to limited maximum speeds. Since the robots’ decisions affect one another, the resulting joint strategy must coordinate their movements over an infinite time horizon. The objective is to minimize the worst-case latency throughout the mission. This problem has attracted significant attention over the past two decades (Machado et al. 2003; Chevaleyre 2004; Portugal and Rocha 2011; Huang et al. 2019; Basilico 2022). Earlier studies formalized the worst-latency objective and showed that computing an optimal monitoring strategy is both NP-hard and APX-hard. A variety of algorithmic approaches have since been proposed, including cycle-based patrol strategies, graph-partition methods, and so on. More recently, learning-based approaches have also been explored to address complex environments. Although these efforts have substantially advanced our understanding of multi-robot persistent monitoring, several fundamental questions remain open. In this work, we investigate the persistent monitoring problem and introduce a family of tail-performance objectives that capture long-run system behavior beyond the initial transient phase, while also generalizing the standard worst-latency objective. We then carry out a theoretical analysis of the problem and establish several key results, including the existence and structural properties of optimal solutions, the validity of periodic approximations, and the soundness of discretizing waiting times at locations. A central consequence of this analysis is that the original infinite-horizon functional optimization problem can be reformulated as an equivalent event-driven Markov decision

Prior work: The persistent monitoring problem has been widely studied in the literature and often appears under related names such as persistent surveillance, coverage, and patrolling. In the simplest single-robot setting with uniform node priorities , Chevaleyre (2004) proves that an optimal policy under the worst-latency criterion is a cyclic tour, and computing such a tour is equivalent to solving the classical NP-hard Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) (Karp 2010). This shows that the problem is already computationally challenging even in its most basic form. For metric TSP, the classical Christofides algorithm achieves a 3/2-approximation guarantee (Christofides 2022). This guarantee was later slightly improved by Karlin et al. (2021). Moreover, Karpinski et al. (2015) show that there is no polynomial-time

The authors analyze the structure of optimal solutions under different recharging requirements and develop corresponding efficient algorithms. Beyond these uniform-priority formulations, realistic monitoring tasks often involve locations with different levels of importance, urgency, or risk, naturally leading to weighted graph models. For multi-robot monitoring , early work focuses primarily on heuristic strategies (Agmon et al. 2011; Portugal and Rocha 2013a,b, 2016; Farinelli et al. 2017; Goeckner et al. 2024a). Despite these advances, many fundamental theoretical questions in multi-robot monitoring remain largely open. In particular, while strong structural and optimality results have been established for the single-robot case (Alamdari et al. 2014), extending such results to the multi-robot setting is highly non-trivial and remains open. Since the persistent monitoring problem can also be naturally viewed as a sequential decision-making problem, it can in principle be addressed with reinforcement learning. With the rapid development of reinforcement learning (Mnih et al. 2015, 2016; Schulman et al. 2017; Hessel et al. 2018; Haarnoja et al. 2018) and multi-agent reinforcement learning (Rashid et al. 2020; Sunehag et al. 2017; De Witt et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2022a; Vinyals et al. 2019), learning-based approaches have been increasingly explored for solving complex single- and multi-robot coordination problems. Early studies mainly rely on tabular methods. Santana et al. (2004) propose two tabular Q-learning-based schemes, namely BBLA and GBLA, which design state representations based on local latency information. This work is later extended in (Lauri and Koukam 2014) by incorporating additional coordination-aware features. More recent approaches have turned to deep reinforcement learning in order to handle larger-scale and more complex environments. Representative methods include value-based approaches such as DQN (Jana et al. 2022), as well as policy-gradient-based methods such as PPO and its multi-agent variants (Guo et al. 2023; Goeckner et al. 2024b; Palma-Borda et al. 2026). In particular, graph neural networks (GNNs) have been increasingly adopted to capture the graph structure of the environment and the interactions among agents. However, despite their empirical success, most existing approaches rely on heuristic reward designs that are only loosely connected to the original monitoring objectives. In particular, for minimizing worst-case latency, commonly used reward functions, such as instantaneous latency or its normalized variants, do not faithfully represent the true objective and may therefore lead to suboptimal policies with respect to the actual performance metric. This limitation largely stems from a fundamental mismatch between the problem objective and the standard MDP formulations. Specifically, classical RL objectives, including discounted cumulative reward and average reward, are based on additive criteria over time, whereas worst-case latency is inherently a non-additive and highly nonlinear functional of the trajectory. To address this gap, we aim to construct an MDP formulation that is fully equivalent to the original persistent monitoring problem under the worst-case latency criterion. In this way, solving the resulting MDP yields an optimal policy for the original problem directly, rather than relying on surrogate reward approximations. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no unified platform that integrates both RL-based and heuristic monitoring methods within a common framework for training, evaluation, and fair performance comparison. Beyond latency-based formulations, the persistent moni-toring literature also encompasses several related but distinct modeling and solution paradigms. One important line of work considers dynamic environments where uncertainty accumulates over time, and the objective is to reduce its long-term growth through persistent coverage (Smith et al. 2012; Cassandras et al. 2013; Zhou et al. 2018; Palacios-Gasós et al. 2019). In these formulations, robots continu-ously influence the environment through their motion and sensing actions, and the problem is therefore often modeled as a continuous-time control problem in which trajecto-ries, speeds, or dwell times are optimized. Another related line studies stochastic patrolling strategies, often based on Markov chains (Duan and Bullo 2021), to achieve desirable properties such as efficiency (Patel et al. 2016; Díaz-García et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2025b), unpredictability (George et al. 2019; Duan et al. 2020, 2026; Wang et al. 2025a), or trade-offs between the two (Grace and Baillieul 2005; Wang et al. 2024). Related game-theoretic formulations have also been considered for designing patrol strategies against prespecified intruder models (Alpern et al. 2011; Asghar and Smith 2018; Duan et al. 2021; Klaška et al. 2021; Darlington et al. 2023; Nagórko et al. 2024; John et al. 2025). 

Contributions: Our core contributions are as follows: 1. We introduce a family of tail-performance objectives for multi-robot persistent monitoring on weighted graphs. This formulation generalizes the conventional worst-case weighted latency criterion by excluding an initial transient phase, thereby capturing the long-run monitoring performance of robot teams under fixed initial deployments. 2. We establish theoretical properties of the proposed objective family and the associated strategy classes. In particular, we prove the existence of optimal strategies, invariance of the optimal value with respect to the transient horizon, monotonicity of optimal strategy sets, reachability of optimal long-run performance from arbitrary initial configurations, approximation by periodic solutions, and bounded performance loss under discretized waiting times. 3. We reformulate the original continuous-time trajectory optimization problem as an equivalent event-driven Markov decision process, termed the TWLO-MDP. By augmenting the state with a historical worst-latency tracker and an elapsed-time counter, the TWLO-MDP transforms the non-additive tail objective into a standard average-reward criterion while preserving optimality. 4. We develop reinforcement-learning-based solution methods for the TWLO-MDP. The proposed learning pipeline incorporates several problem-specific training

## 3.2 Arbitrarily close approximation via periodic solutions 

Designing monitoring strategies over an infinite horizon is highly challenging, since one needs to plan the motion of multiple robots over the entire infinite time axis, and the performance metric depends on the complete sequence of visits. ## 4.2 Reinforcement-Learning-Based Solution Methods 

Having reformulated the original monitoring problem as the equivalent TWLO-MDP, whose optimal policy can be directly converted into an optimal monitoring strategy, we next introduce practical reinforcement-learning-based solution methods for solving this MDP. Since the reformulated problem is an infinite-horizon average-reward MDP, whereas practical training must be conducted on truncated finite-length episodes, we approximate it using long rollouts together with a large discount factor γ. This leads to a discounted-reward formulation that is compatible with widely used state-of-the-art RL algorithms. Within the proposed framework, we implement and evaluate multiple RL algorithms in Section 5. For concreteness, we use MAPPO (Yu et al. 2022a) as a representative example in the following, since it consistently demonstrates strong and stable performance in our experiments. Based on this instantiation, we then describe the key design choices and training techniques adopted in our method.

Binary readiness flags and categorical one-hot identity encodings are already bounded in {0, 1} and do not encode magnitude information, so they are passed through without modification.

Parameter sharing and role information. To improve scalability and sample efficiency, we adopt parameter sharing across all agents, which is common in MARL (Foerster et al. 2016; Grammel et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2022b). This choice is also well aligned with our monitoring problem, where the robots are homogeneous and are expected to follow a common decision rule. However, parameter sharing introduces an inherent symmetry issue. To provide sufficient decision information, each agent is allowed to access the global state. Yet, when all agents receive exactly the same global input under a shared network, the policy cannot distinguish which agent is being controlled. A natural remedy is to additionally provide each agent’s own position. Nevertheless, this remains insufficient in our setting: if two agents arrive at the same location and observe the same global state, then under a shared network and deterministic environment dynamics, a deterministic policy may drive them to take identical actions thereafter, leading to severe redundancy and inefficient use of monitoring resources. A common way to break this symmetry is to augment the input with an agent-specific index, as is standard in parameter-sharing MARL (Foerster et al. 2018; Rashid et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2022a). Although such a design can improve coordination without fundamentally reducing policy expressiveness, it also breaks the permutation symmetry inherent in the original monitoring problem. To address this issue, we introduce a lightweight role encoding that distinguishes agents while preserving symmetry as much as possible. Specifically, instead of assigning each agent a fixed identity index, we augment its positional information with a local decision-order label, defined as the order in which the agent arrives at the current node among all agents that are ready to act there. 

Imitation learning. Pure reinforcement learning can be inefficient for our monitoring problem, especially in large-scale environments with multiple agents, since effective policies typically require nontrivial coordination patterns and often exhibit strong structural regularity. In such complex multi-agent settings, discovering these strategies purely through exploration is challenging, particularly when the reward is sparse, delayed, and coupled across agents. Motivated by the widespread use of imitation learning as a warm-start mechanism for difficult RL problems (Rajeswaran et al. 2018; Salimans and Chen 2018; Hester et al. 2018; Vinyals et al. 2019), we combine imitation learning with subsequent RL. As outlined in Algorithm 1, this approach provides a strong initial policy prior from heuristic demonstrations, while still allowing RL to further improve the policy beyond the original heuristic. Specifically, we first run heuristic policies to collect demonstration trajectories and compute normalized Monte Carlo returns from Line 4 to 7. These tuples are subsequently stored in an offline dataset D at Line 8. Based on this dataset, we pretrain both the actor and the critic before RL optimization. As detailed in Line 14, the actor is pretrained via behavior cloning on the demonstrated actions using a cross-entropy loss, while enforcing the corresponding action masks min to ensure only valid actions are considered. To remain consistent with the value normalization (Yu et al. 2022a) used in the subsequent RL stage, Line 13 shows that the critic is trained to fit the normalized returns ˆGin via mean squared error. The corresponding mean E[G] and standard deviation std( G) are then utilized to initialize the running return statistics during the RL training phase in Line 18. In this way, imitation learning provides a strong warm start for the policy and aligns the critic initialization with the normalized value learning used later in MAPPO. Following the same paradigm, we extended this imitation learning pretraining mechanism to other MARL algorithms, with empirical evaluations demonstrating that MAPPO yields the best performance. 

## 5 Platform 

Having established the theoretical foundations and devel-oped the RL-based solution methods for the multi-robot persistent monitoring problem, we now present a modu-lar and extensible simulation and evaluation platform that integrates representative heuristics and RL-based algorithms within a unified framework. The platform is designed to support direct and fair comparison across algorithms under shared benchmark settings, while allowing easy incorpo-ration of newly developed methods. Such a platform is needed because, although a wide range of methods has been proposed for multi-robot monitoring, systematic com-parison among these methods remains limited. As dis-cussed in the related work section, several existing plat-forms integrate representative heuristic algorithms, such as 

patrolling_sim (Portugal et al. 2018). However, to the best of our knowledge, no unified platform currently supports both heuristic and RL-based monitoring methods in a manner that enables direct comparison under unified environments, interfaces, and evaluation criteria. To address this gap, we develop Multi-Robot Monitoring Benchmark (M2Bench) , a modular and extensible simulation platform for persistent monitoring. As illustrated in Fig. 2, M2Bench structures the experimental workflow into several modular layers. These include a YAML-driven configuration layer, an environment layer for shared monitoring dynamics and task-specific MDP wrappers, a data layer for rollout collection and replay storage, an algorithm layer that integrates heuristic and learning-based methods, and a utility layer for evaluation,

Modular Design and Configuration Workflow. Building on this layered architecture, M2Bench represents each part of an experiment as an interchangeable module with a clearly defined interface. Users can modify or replace individual components, including the graph instance, monitoring formulation, policy class, algorithm, and neural network architecture, without rewriting the remaining experimental pipeline. The platform builds on widely used libraries, including PyTorch (Paszke et al. 2019), Gymnasium (Towers et al. 2024), and PettingZoo (Terry et al. 2021), while avoiding dependence on a heavy monolithic training framework. From the users’ perspective, these modules are assembled through a unified configuration-driven workflow. A single YAML file specifies the graph, monitoring or MDP formulation, robot settings, algorithm type, network options, and training hyperparameters. Given this configuration, M2Bench automatically instantiates the corresponding environment, policy, collector, buffer, trainer, and evaluation routines, and executes the associated experimental pipeline, including training, checkpointing, testing, quantitative evaluation, logging with tools such as W&B and TensorBoard, visualization, animation rendering, and hyperparameter sweeps. This declarative interface facilitates switching between heuristic and RL-based methods, comparing different monitoring formulations under shared settings, and reproducing experiments without repeated modifications to low-level code. 

Monitoring-Oriented Simulation. The core environment component of M2Bench is MonitorWorld , which simulates persistent monitoring dynamics on finite weighted graphs. Following Section 2.1, it models robot motion, waiting, travel, visit events, physical-time evolution, and latency updates. MonitorWorld is independent of specific MDP formulations. Observations, action encodings, rewards, and termination conditions are instead defined by external wrappers, allowing multiple learning formulations and non-RL heuristic policies to share the same simulator. It supports both fixed-step synchronous simulation and event-driven asynchronous simulation, enabling discrete-time and event-driven MDPs to be built on a common environment. This shared design also facilitates imitation pretraining in Section 4.2, since heuristic trajectories can be directly reused by learning-based methods.

## 6 Experiments 

In this section, we present an empirical evaluation of the proposed framework in two parts. We first examine our main methodological contribution, namely the equivalent monitoring MDP and its associated learning pipeline, and validate its effectiveness across several monitoring scenarios. We then situate the proposed method within a broader benchmarking context by comparing heuristic and learning-based approaches under the unified M2Bench platform, thereby demonstrating both the practical advantages of our method and the platform’s ability to support integrated evaluation. 

## 6.1 Learning Performance 

We first evaluate the effectiveness of the learning methods introduced in Section 4.2. Using M2Bench, we compare several representative solution algorithms under identical monitoring environments and evaluation protocols. The goal of this experiment is twofold: to determine whether the proposed MDP-based learning pipeline can produce effective monitoring policies, and to characterize the behavior of different MARL algorithms under the proposed monitoring formulation. The results shown in Fig. 3 demonstrate that the proposed monitoring MDP can be effectively optimized by representative MARL algorithms. On the long-edge graph, policy-gradient-based methods, including MAPPO, IPPO, and VDPPO, rapidly reduce the worst weighted latency and converge to low values. By contrast, IQL performs substantially worse, indicating that independent value learning is inadequate for this cooperative persistent monitoring task. Value-decomposition methods such as VDN and QMIX also improve over training, but they exhibit slower convergence and slightly worse performance. The San Francisco crime-map scenario presents a more challenging setting and results in more pronounced performance differences. In this setting, MAPPO with imitation-based initialization achieves the best final performance and outperforms MAPPO trained from scratch, despite noticeable fluctuations in the early training stage. This result suggests that heuristic demonstrations provide useful prior information for MARL fine-tuning, especially in complex monitoring environments. Taken together, these results validate both the learnability of the proposed MDP formulation and the effectiveness of the associated learning pipeline. 

## 6.2 Benchmark Comparison on M2Bench 

We further conduct a unified benchmark comparison with the algorithms integrated in M2Bench. In contrast to the previous experiment, which focuses on representative MARL algorithms, this comparison covers both the proposed learning-based method and several classical monitoring baselines summarized in Table 1. The purpose is to evaluate different algorithmic paradigms under identical environments, metrics, and execution protocols, and to assess whether the proposed method remains competitive with established heuristic policies. For clarity, algorithms that fail to converge or exhibit severe instability within the training budget are omitted from the plotted curves. The results are shown in Fig. 4. On the long-edge graph, the proposed MAPPO-TWLO method achieves the lowest worst-case weighted latency among the compared algorithms and converges to a stable low value. Several RL-based methods, such as MAGEC, BBLA, OUCS, and S4R1, also produce reasonable monitoring performance, but their final latency values remain noticeably higher than that of MAPPO-TWLO. Other methods, such as EXGBLA and NEP, stay at substantially larger latency levels, indicating that their decision rules are less suitable for this graph structure. These results suggest that the proposed learning-based method effectively leverages the event-driven MDP formulation to learn a monitoring policy that outperforms representative heuristic baselines in this controlled benchmark. In the San Francisco crime-map scenario, the performance gap among different algorithms becomes more pronounced. This setting is more challenging than the synthetic long-edge graph, as it involves a realistic weighted graph with nonuniform node priorities and heterogeneous edge lengths. MAPPO-TWLO with imitation-based initialization achieves the best final performance, while MAPPO-TWLO trained from scratch also surpasses most heuristic baselines after sufficient training. By contrast, several heuristic methods remain at substantially higher latency levels or display persistent fluctuations. These results indicate that fixed heuristic decision rules may have limited adaptability in complex weighted monitoring environments, whereas the proposed learning pipeline can adapt the policy to the task-specific graph structure and priority distribution.

## 7 Conclusion 

This paper studied multi-robot persistent monitoring on weighted graphs under a tail worst-case weighted-latency objective. We introduced a family of tail-performance objectives to capture long-run monitoring quality beyond the initial transient phase, and established several theoretical results, including optimal-solution existence, invariance of the optimal value, periodic approximation, and bounded error under discretized waiting times. We then reformulated the original continuous-time problem as an equivalent event-driven Markov decision process, termed the TWLO-MDP. This reformulation converts the non-additive tail objective into a standard average-reward criterion while preserving optimality, thereby enabling reinforcement-learning-based solution methods. We further developed M2Bench, a unified platform for training, evaluating, and comparing heuristic and learning-based monitoring algorithms. Experiments on synthetic and realistic scenarios demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed methods. Future work will consider more complicated monitoring settings, including dynamic graphs, uncertain travel times, changing node priorities, and additional robot constraints, as well as improved scalability to larger teams and environments.
