# A Quantitative Framework for Navigating Controller Design Tradeoffs under Computational Constraints 

Chris Verhoek and Nikolai Matni ∗

April 29, 2026 

Abstract 

Computational constraints permeate the controller design process, and yet are rarely treated as explicit design constraints. Towards addressing this gap, we propose a quantitative framework that captures the effects of common design approximations, such as model order reduction, tem-poral discretization, horizon truncation, and solver accuracy, on both controller performance and computational requirements. Our framework highlights that these approximations are tunable parameters within an overall controller design process. By leveraging incremental input-to-state stability, we show that bounding the aggregate effects of these approximations reduces to ver-ifying a design-dependent sector bound on the difference between the deployed policy and an idealized baseline, with stability enforced via a small-gain condition. We operationalize these insights via a Design Meta-Problem in which the performance gap is minimized subject to sta-bility, real-time compute, and timing constraints. Finally, we instantiate the framework on a receding horizon LQR case study, and demonstrate a principled near-optimal navigation of tradeoffs among sampling rate, model order, horizon length, and solver iterations. 

# 1 Introduction 

Controllers implemented on digital hardware must respect hard timing bounds imposed by plant dynamics, directly coupling achievable performance to available processing speed. Yet computa-tional limits are rarely treated as first-class design constraints. Instead, tractability is achieved through a sequence of standard simplifications falling into roughly three categories: (i) modeling approximations, e.g., discretization of continuous-time dynamics, truncation of infinite horizons, and model order reduction, (ii) problem relaxations, e.g., convex relaxations of nonconvex optimal control problems, and early termination of iterative solvers, and (iii) architectural decompositions, e.g., layered control hierarchies that distribute computation across decision-making, planning, and regulation layers. While each simplification is individually well-motivated, they are typically introduced in iso-lation, and their combined effect on stability, performance, and computational feasibility is rarely analyzed jointly. 2.1 Design Approximations 

1) Reduced Order Models and Policy Classes: It is common to approximate the dynamics with a reduced order model ˆf from a model class F (e.g., linear, control-affine), and similarly to restrict our search to policies ˆ π lying in a policy class Π (e.g., Markovian, time-invariant). We denote the joint reduced order model and policy class by M = F × Π. 

2) Horizon Length: Rather than solving problem (2) over an infinite horizon, it is instead solved over a finite horizon T . The cost for t ∈ (T, ∞) is captured by augmenting the finite-horizon objective with a terminal cost cT (xT ). 

3) Temporal Discretization: To make solving the optimal control problem (2) computation-ally tractable, it is often temporally discretized with a sampling time τ .

4) Optimization Iterations: The resulting reduced-order, finite-horizon, discrete-time prob-lem, is often deployed in a receding horizon fashion, which demands online re-solving at each step using iterative algorithms (e.g., gradient descent, interior point methods). Due to loop rate and compute constraints, only a finite number N of iterations can be run. 

Remark 1. This list is not exhaustive, and the proposed framework can easily be extended to handle other bespoke approximations and design choices. Moreover, the order in which these design approximations are introduced may depend on the control design problem at hand. While these approximations are necessary to make the control design problem (2) tractable, the resulting deployed policy ˆ π must still respect certain key design constraints .

32.2 Design Constraints 

1) Stability Constraints: A minimal requirement is for the deployed policy ˆ π ∈ Π to be stabilizing. This, in turn, may impose lower bounds on model and policy class complexity M, on loop rate and sampling time, on horizon length, and on the number of optimization iterations. 

2) Performance Constraints: These may be imposed from the design problem at hand or due to integration within a larger LCA. These could include, e.g., tracking error or convergence rate guarantees. These can be enforced by requiring a policy ˆ π satisfying R(ˆ π) ≤ 0, for a suitably defined requirements function R.

3) Offline Compute Constraints: While heavier demands can be made of offline compute requirements, we still require methods that can be solved within a prescribed amount of time, e.g., polynomial time synthesis algorithms. 

4) Online Compute Constraints: Online computation must respect constraints as imposed by, e.g., loop rate constraints and hardware capabilities. As before, this list is not exhaustive, and our framework naturally accommodates additional constraints. 

2.3 Problem Statement 

While the above design approximations and constraints are well understood individually, they have yet to be holistically quantified and optimized. We therefore seek to provide a systematic framework for navigating the tradeoffs introduced by these design approximations and constraints. Toward that end, in the next section, we formulate a DMP for the optimal control problem (2) that optimizes the performance gap of the deployed policy induced by the design approximations of §2.1 subject to the constraints of §2.2, with a particular emphasis on explicitly encoding computational constraints .

# 3 The Design Meta-Problem 

To track the contribution of each approximation to the overall performance gap, we associate a dedicated approximate policy with each design parameter, wherein subscripts identify the relevant quantities that define the approximation. For example, ˆ πM denotes the policy computed using the reduced order model and policy class M, ˆ πT denotes the policy computed for a finite horizon of length T , ˆ πτ denotes the policy computed using a sampling time τ , and ˆ πN denotes the policy computed using N compute iterations. There is an implicit dependence induced by the order of the approximations, e.g., ˆ πN here is inherently dependent on the choices of τ, T, M, i.e., ˆ πN,τ,T, M.However, to reduce notational clutter, we suppress this implicit dependence in our notation. In our model, the deployed policy (on f ) is ˆ πN , and hence our goal is to understand the effect of the design choices on the performance gap E := J(ˆ πN ) − J(π⋆). 

Remark 2. We use the optimal policy π⋆ as a natural baseline, but this is not a requirement, and ˆπ can be compared against any baseline policy. This is particularly useful when π⋆ is impossible to compute, but a performant yet non-implementable policy (e.g., one that violates online compute constraints) is available as a baseline. # 4 Formalizing the Design Meta-Problem via Incremental Input-to-State Stability 

Towards simplifying exposition, we assume that problem (2) is time-invariant, i.e., ct ≡ c, ft ≡ f ,

Xt ≡ X , and Ut ≡ U , so that the optimal baseline policy π⋆ is Markovian and time-invariant. Although time-varying policies may be optimal for sub-problems induced by design approximations, we will consider the deployment of these solutions in a receding horizon fashion, and hence, we can assume without loss of generality that ˆ πt(x[0 ,t ], u [0 ,t )) = ˆ π(xt). To bound the performance gap of ˆ π, we view it as a perturbed version of the baseline policy 

π⋆, i.e., ˆ π(x) = π⋆(x) + ∆( x), where ∆( x) := (ˆ π − π⋆)( x) denotes the policy perturbation. Towards that end, for a fixed policy u = π(x), define the closed-loop dynamics f π(x, u ) := f (x, π (x) + u), and f π(x) := f π(x, 0). To characterize the difference in cost achieved by the baseline closed-loop system f ⋆ := f π⋆

and a closed-loop system evolving under the deployed policy ˆ π, i.e., ˆf := f ˆπ, we observe that ˆf (ˆ x) = f ⋆(ˆ x, ∆(ˆ x)), i.e., it is an input perturbed version of the baseline system f ⋆.

Remark 3. Throughout, we use ( x, x ′) and ( f, f ′) to denote generic nominal and perturbed sys-tems, reserving ( x⋆, ˆx) and ( f ⋆, ˆf ) for the baseline and deployed systems specifically. The former appear in the general results of §4.2, while the latter connect those to formalizing the DMP (4). Incremental input-to-state stability, which characterizes a notion of robust stability between trajectories, provides a natural framework for bounding the effects of such input perturbations on closed-loop behavior, see Angeli (2002) and references therein. We show next that if the baseline 6system f ⋆ is δISS, then stability and performance guarantees can then be characterized via a suitable small-gain condition defined in terms of a tunable sector bound on the policy difference.

# 6 Conclusions and Outlook 

We presented a framework for systematically navigating approximation choices in controller de-sign under explicit computational constraints, formalized as a Design Meta-Problem (DMP) that jointly optimizes controller performance subject to stability, real-time compute, and timing con-straints. When the baseline system is δISS, bounding closed-loop performance degradation reduces to verifying a design-dependent sector bound on the policy difference, with stability enforced via a small-gain condition. In the receding horizon LQR setting, this yields a scalar optimization over τ for each candidate model Mi, from which a jointly optimal ( τ ⋆, M ⋆i ) is identified by direct comparison. 15 Looking ahead, we aim to extend the framework in three directions. First, for the LQR example, the sector bound gains ˆL, L ⋆ were estimated by bounding errors in the feedback gains for various choices of τ . While this is tractable linear feedback policies, it may not be in general, and developing general data-driven or empirical methods for fitting these gains, e.g., via sampling-based estimation of ∥(ˆ π − π⋆)( x)∥, is an important direction for broader applicability. Second, the DMP naturally accommodates additional approximations and constraints, such as memory constraints for explicit MPC or sector bound terms capturing model uncertainty from data-driven identification. Third, and more ambitiously, Theorem 1 provides a principled building block for the systematic design of layered control architectures Matni et al. (2024): when a single-layer DMP is infeasible, this motivates decomposing the problem into layers, each with its own compute resources and DMP, linked via inter-layer constraints.
